Abstract

This project learns Goofy's way of moving, the hunch, the bent knees, the loose stride, from 71 short clips of his Disney shorts of the 1940s and early 1950s, building on STyMo (SIGGRAPH 2026), which learns from motion capture. Here his way of moving is an explicit quantity: a correction on the skeleton, learned from pairs of his clips and plain performances of the same actions, read alongside the phase of the person's action, and separate from his look. It can be measured per joint, applied to any performance, including actions he never did on film, and mapped onto a robot. A video model learns motion and look together, in one latent under one objective, with no separate quantity for manner. The output is a 3D motion file that keeps the person's action and timing: a video model can paint him back into the original footage, and a humanoid robot can walk it in simulation.

Method Overview

His clips come from 45 of his films, where scene detection found 3,812 scenes and shots. Of those, 657 were judged by hand and 64 kept, giving 71 training pairs and four held out to check the model. His leg joints were marked by hand on 141 drawings and corrected on 120 more, and a detector trained on those marks placed the rest of the 764. When each foot is down was judged by hand on 475 drawings of 19 clips, and set by rule elsewhere.

The split of a style into a posture and a way of moving through it is STyMo's. Here the posture is one fixed pose, measured from his films and frozen by hand, and only the way of moving is learned: once, from the 71 pairs, 195 seconds of his film, in 103 minutes on one GPU.

Every frame is labelled with one of ten kinds of movement, read from the person's motion, not his.

Most kinds appear briefly inside clips that are mostly something else: a turn shows up in 30 clips but only 9 percent of the frames.

*The tokenizer split these by how they move, not by what they are.

At run time a new performance, captured, generated or read off video, takes on the fixed pose, then the correction, then a head rule that gives him the person's gaze in place of the model's own head, then one solve that keeps it on the person's path and footing.

What's next

  • More data where it's thin. A model can only learn a manner from moves it has examples of, and the table above shows how uneven they are. So far his manner shows most clearly when the person stands still. More walks, runs, jumps and kicks, from more of his film or from generated performances of him, would show whether it carries into those moves too.
  • Anticipation. The small wind-up before an action is one of the classic principles of animation, and a large part of how Goofy moves. The labels the model reads describe only what the body is doing, so the moment before someone sets off looks like standing. Labelling moments by intent, such as 'about to set off', would let the model learn the wind-up and add it before an action.
  • Better pairs. Each training example pairs his clip with a plain performance of the same action, but two performances differ in more than manner: a different step, a different hand. The model learns those differences as style too. Building the plain version out of his own clip would leave only the manner to learn.
  • Into animation pipelines. Animators already work in layers. The model's correction behaves like an additive animation layer, so it could be delivered as one, dialled up or down and masked per limb in an animator's own tools. And the pairs, sorted by kind of movement, have the shape of a motion-matching library: with enough clips, his own moves could be looked up for the moment the person is in.

Technical notes
Goofy is a Disney character; his films are the Disney shorts of the 1940s and early 1950s, used here as the subject of study.
Reading his pose: FlowHMR (Wang et al., "FlowHMR: Physically Plausible Motion Capture from Video", 2026) and GVHMR (Shen et al., "World-Grounded Human Motion Recovery via Gravity-View Coordinates", SIGGRAPH Asia 2024), on the SMPL-X body model (Pavlakos et al., "Expressive Body Capture: 3D Hands, Face, and Body from a Single Image", CVPR 2019).
The plain performances in training: Kimodo (Rempe et al., NVIDIA, "Kimodo: Scaling Controllable Human Motion Generation", 2026) and HY-Motion 1.0 (Tencent Hunyuan, "HY-Motion 1.0: Scaling Flow Matching Models for Text-To-Motion Generation", 2025).
The phase labels: MoMask (Guo et al., "MoMask: Generative Masked Modeling of 3D Human Motions", CVPR 2024), trained on HumanML3D (Guo et al., "Generating Diverse and Natural 3D Human Motions from Text", CVPR 2022), whose motions come from AMASS.
New inputs: the shot put and robot walk from AMASS's Eyes Japan and KIT sets (AMASS: Mahmood et al., "AMASS: Archive of Motion Capture as Surface Shapes", ICCV 2019; Eyes Japan: mocapdata.com, Eyes, JAPAN Co. Ltd.; KIT: Mandery et al., "Unifying Representations and Large-Scale Whole-Body Motion Databases for Studying Human Motion", IEEE Transactions on Robotics 2016); the ballet from HY-Motion 1.0; the person videos for the golf swing, bus stop, kick and skid generated with Seedance 1.5 pro (ByteDance) and read with GVHMR.
The model builds on STyMo: Ponton, Winkler, Kavan, Ye and Kadlecek, "STyMo: Fast and Controllable Few-Shot Motion Style Transfer", SIGGRAPH 2026 (project page).
The video model: Wan 2.7 (Alibaba).
The robot: the Unitree G1, under NVIDIA's GEAR-SONIC balance controller (Luo et al., "SONIC: Supersizing motion tracking for natural humanoid whole-body control", Science Robotics 2026), the motions mapped onto it with GMR (Araujo et al., "Retargeting Matters: General Motion Retargeting for Humanoid Motion Tracking", 2025), simulated in MuJoCo.
